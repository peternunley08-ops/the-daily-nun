When does a recommendation become manipulation?

A feed is a ranking. The question is what it ranks for, and whether the person scrolling would agree.

By Peter Nunley  /  2 minute read

Nobody scrolls through everything. Out of millions of posts, a system picks the few that appear first. Companies describe that system one way. Researchers who test it sometimes find something else.

A click measures behavior. It does not measure approval.

What the companies say

TikTok, Instagram and YouTube each say their systems predict behavior: what a person will watch, finish, like, comment on or share. YouTube says recommendations drive "a significant amount" of its viewing, without a number. These are company statements from 2020 to 2023, not independent audits.

What one audit found

In February 2023, researchers compared Twitter's engagement-ranked timeline with a plain newest-first timeline for the same 806 U.S. users. The ranked feed "amplifies emotionally charged, out-group hostile content," they wrote, and users rated its political picks lower than the newest-first ones.

A separate 2020 election study moved consenting Facebook and Instagram users to newest-first feeds for three months. They spent less time on the apps, but their polarization and political knowledge did not measurably change.

Who benefits when a post makes people angry?

A study of 2.7 million posts by news outlets and members of Congress found that posts about the other political side were shared about twice as often as posts about one's own side. The poster gains reach. The FTC has described platforms' business models as built on targeted advertising, but no source here shows that anger in particular earns them more. The authors say social media "may be creating perverse incentives." That is their reading, with a "may."

Does it change what people buy, or who they trust?

  • Buying. In a 2022 Pew survey, three in ten adult social media users said they had bought something after an influencer's post; 41% of those under 30. A field experiment found personality-matched ads produced "up to 50% more purchases," a best case, not an average.
  • Candidates. Feeds carry politics: 37% of adults 18 to 29 regularly get news from news influencers, Pew found in 2024. But experiments on ads and on quitting Facebook or Instagram before the 2016 and 2020 elections mostly found small or near-zero effects on candidate support and turnout.

Where the line might be

This part is interpretation. Three scholars define persuasion as "the forthright appeal to another person's decision-making power" and manipulation as "hidden influence." By that test, three questions matter: Is the ranking open about what it is doing? Does it match what the person says they want? Can the person turn it off?

What is not known

The research covers adults, ads and whole platforms. None of it isolates a ranking system's effect on high-school students, or on beliefs over years. ■

The short version

  • Feeds rank by predicted behavior, and one audit found the ranked feed favored hostile posts users did not prefer.
  • Studies measured some effect on buying. Effects on candidate support were mostly small.
  • By one scholarly definition, the test is whether a feed is open, wanted and refusable.
