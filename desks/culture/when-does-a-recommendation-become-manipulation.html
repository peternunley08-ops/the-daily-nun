When does a recommendation become manipulation?

By Peter Nunley  ·  Draft for review

Nobody scrolls through everything. A feed is a ranking: out of millions of possible videos and posts, a system decides which few appear first. The question is what the system is ranking for, and whether the person scrolling would agree with the answer.

The Daily Nun read what three large platforms say about their own systems, then compared those statements with independent research. The two kinds of source are kept separate below, because a company describing its product is not the same as a researcher testing it.

What the companies say

These are corporate statements. They have not been independently audited, and the oldest is from 2020.

TikTok says its For You feed weighs "user interactions such as the videos you like or share, accounts you follow, comments you post, and content you create," and gives strong weight to whether a user finishes a longer video. Source: How TikTok recommends videos #ForYou, June 18, 2020.

Instagram says its Feed ranking predicts "how likely you are to spend a few seconds on a post, comment on it, like it, share it." Source: Instagram Ranking Explained, by Adam Mosseri, May 31, 2023.

YouTube says its system uses "clicks, watchtime, survey responses, sharing, likes, and dislikes," and that "recommendations drive a significant amount of the overall viewership on YouTube." It gives no number. Source: On YouTube's recommendation system, by Cristos Goodrow, September 15, 2021.

The three descriptions share one idea. Each system predicts behavior: what a person will watch, finish, like or share. All three companies also describe controls, such as marking a post "Not Interested," and YouTube says it uses viewer surveys alongside watch time.

What independent research found

One peer-reviewed audit tested the gap between engagement and preference directly. Researchers recruited 806 Twitter users in the United States between February 11 and 27, 2023, and compared the platform's engagement-ranked timeline with a plain newest-first timeline for the same people. Source: Milli et al., PNAS Nexus, March 5, 2025.

The authors report that the engagement-based ranking "amplifies emotionally charged, out-group hostile content." Tweets expressing anger rose by 0.47 standard deviations, and partisanship and hostility toward the other side each rose by 0.24. The same users rated the political tweets the algorithm chose lower than the political tweets in the newest-first timeline.

That study has limits. It covers one platform at one moment. Its sample was recruited through a crowd-work site and skewed younger and more Democratic than the country. It measured what people saw and how they rated it, not whether their views changed over time.

A second study points in a different direction. During the 2020 U.S. election, researchers working with Meta moved consenting Facebook and Instagram users to newest-first feeds for three months. Those users spent less time on the platforms, but the study found no significant change in polarization or political knowledge over that period. Source: Guess et al., Science, July 27, 2023. The Daily Nun read this study's abstract only.

Read together, the two studies support a narrow claim. Ranking by engagement can change what appears in a feed, and it can favor posts that people do not say they want. Whether that changes what people believe is not settled by this evidence.

Does a feed change what people buy?

The evidence here is about advertising and creators, not about ranking systems directly, and it covers adults.

In a Pew Research Center survey of 6,034 U.S. adults taken July 5 to 17, 2022, three in ten adult social media users said they had bought something after seeing an influencer or content creator post about it. Among users under 30, the share was 41%. Among users who follow influencers, it was 53%. Source: Pew Research Center, November 21, 2022. Those are self-reports. They show that people connect purchases to posts, not that the post caused a purchase that would not have happened anyway.

One experiment tested cause more directly. Across three field experiments on Facebook that reached more than 3.5 million people, ads written to match a person's predicted personality produced "up to 40% more clicks and up to 50% more purchases" than mismatched or unpersonalized ads. Source: Matz et al., PNAS, November 13, 2017. "Up to" marks the best case in the study, not the average, and the products were ordinary consumer goods. The Daily Nun read this study's abstract only. The authors themselves raise "potential pitfalls related to manipulation and privacy."

So tailored content can move some buying. How much of everyday spending a feed accounts for is not something these sources measure.

Does a feed change which candidate people trust?

Feeds are a real source of political information. In a Pew survey of 10,658 U.S. adults taken July 15 to August 4, 2024, 21% said they regularly get news from news influencers on social media. Among adults 18 to 29, it was 37%. Source: Pew Research Center, November 18, 2024.

Seeing political content is different from being moved by it, and the experiments on that question mostly find small effects. The Daily Nun read abstracts only for the three studies below.

  • In 2016, researchers tested 49 presidential campaign ads in 59 experiments on 34,000 people and found "small average effects on candidate favorability and vote," for Democrats, Republicans and independents alike.
  • In 2020, an $8.9 million social media ad program reached 2 million voters in five battleground states for eight months. The researchers found no evidence that it raised or lowered turnout on average, and shifts within groups of under half a percentage point.
  • Also in 2020, researchers paid 19,857 Facebook users and 15,585 Instagram users to deactivate their accounts for six weeks before the election. Effects on candidate favorability, polarization and turnout were "precisely estimated and close to zero."

Sources, in order: Coppock, Hill and Vavreck, Science Advances, September 2, 2020; Aggarwal et al., Nature Human Behaviour, January 12, 2023; Allcott et al., PNAS, May 13, 2024.

These studies cover weeks or months of one U.S. presidential race, where most voters already know the candidates. They do not test years of exposure, local races with unfamiliar names, or people too young to vote. Small average effects can also still matter in a close election.

Where the line might be

This section is interpretation, not reporting.

Three scholars, Daniel Susser, Beate Roessler and Helen Nissenbaum, offer one definition. Persuasion, they write, is "the forthright appeal to another person's decision-making power." Manipulation is "hidden influence—the covert subversion of another person's decision-making power," and it works by exploiting a person's weaknesses and vulnerabilities. Source: Online Manipulation: Hidden Influences in a Digital World, Georgetown Law Technology Review, January 2020. The authors note that not every philosopher agrees that manipulation must be hidden.

Applied to a feed, their definition suggests three questions rather than one verdict:

  • Is it open? A recommendation labeled as a recommendation, with a stated reason, is a forthright appeal. A ranking the user cannot see or question is closer to hidden influence.
  • Does it follow what the person would endorse? The Twitter audit found a gap between what users engaged with and what they said they preferred.
  • Can the person say no? A control that is hard to find, or absent, leaves the ranking as the only choice.

On that last question, a Federal Trade Commission staff report on nine social media and video companies, released September 19, 2024, said users "had little or no way to opt out of how their data was used by these automated systems," and that the services "often treated teens the same as adult users." Source: FTC press release. That report concerns data practices, not the effect of ranking on beliefs, and The Daily Nun read the press release, not the full report.

What this article does not show

The purchase evidence comes from self-reports and one advertising experiment. The candidate evidence comes from adults in two presidential elections. None of it studies high-school students, and none of it isolates the ranking system from everything else on a platform.

Nothing here shows that a feed controls anyone. People skip, search, unfollow and close the app. The narrower point is that a click is a measurement of behavior, and behavior is not always approval.

Three things a reader can check

  1. Open the "Why you're seeing this post" option where a platform offers one, and compare the stated reason with the post.
  2. Switch to a following-only or newest-first view for a week, where one exists, and notice what changes.
  3. Before sharing a post that provokes anger, ask whether it would have been worth seeking out.
