What happens when the local paper closes?

Fewer reporters cover town councils and school boards. Researchers have measured some of what changes after they leave.

By Peter Nunley  /  1 minute read

Picture a school board meeting with an empty press row. The vote still happens. The budget still passes. The question is who tells everyone else.

The meeting still happens when the reporter is gone. Fewer people are there to write down what was decided.

How much local news is left

Northwestern University's Medill Local News Initiative tracks local news outlets county by county. Its October 13, 2026 report counted 238 U.S. counties with no locally based professional news source, and more than 1,400 more with only one. It also counted 170 newspapers that closed or merged in the year before.

Those counts cover professional local outlets. They do not mean a county has no information at all; residents may still get national news or broadcasts from nearby cities.

What researchers measured

  • Borrowing costs. A 2020 study in the Journal of Financial Economics found that after a local newspaper closed, the local government's borrowing costs rose by 5 to 11 basis points, hundredths of a percentage point. The authors put that at about $650,000 per bond issue, and they also linked closures to higher government wages and deficits. They describe the effect as causal.
  • Knowing your representative. A study of U.S. House districts found that where newspaper markets lined up poorly with district lines, papers covered the representative less. Voters there were less likely to recall their representative's name, and the representatives "work less for their constituencies," the authors wrote.
  • Local swapped for national. When one company bought a wave of local TV stations, a 2019 study found the stations shifted coverage from local politics to national politics, along with a rightward shift in slant. Viewership dipped slightly.

What is not known

These studies describe patterns across many places and earlier years. They do not say what happens in any one town. The borrowing-cost estimate comes from the study's summary; The Daily Nun did not review its full data or methods. ■

The short version

  • 238 U.S. counties have no locally based professional news source, by one 2026 count.
  • Studies link newspaper closures and thinner coverage to higher public borrowing costs and less-informed voters.
  • These studies show patterns across many places, not what happens in any one town.
