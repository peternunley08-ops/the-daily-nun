# Who decides who gets the hours?

*The schedule is the employer's call. The law sets a few limits, and most of them depend on where the job is.*

Imagine the schedule goes up on Thursday night. Saturday's shift, the one already counted toward a phone bill, is gone. Nobody explains why.

For a lot of hourly workers, that is not unusual.

> A schedule is a promise of pay. The rules for changing it depend on where the job is.

## What researchers measured

The Shift Project at Harvard surveyed about 30,000 workers at 120 of the largest U.S. retail and food-service companies. Its October 2019 brief found that two-thirds got less than two weeks' notice of their schedule, and half of those got less than one week.

In the month before the survey, 14% had at least one shift cancelled and 70% had at least one shift's timing changed. A quarter worked on-call shifts. Half had worked a "clopening": closing one night and opening the next morning, less than 11 hours later.

Those workers reported more hunger and money trouble than workers on steadier schedules, even at the same pay. The study shows a link, not proof that the schedules caused the hardship.

## What California law already says

- **Sent home early.** If a worker reports for a scheduled shift and gets less than half of it because there is not enough work, the employer generally owes pay for half the shift: at least two hours, at most four.
- **Students under 18.** A 16- or 17-year-old can work at most four hours on a school day, eight on other days, and 48 hours a week, and needs a work permit from school.
- **Advance notice depends on the city.** Los Angeles requires large retail chains to post schedules 14 days ahead and pay extra for late changes. That rule covers chains with 300 or more employees, and only inside Los Angeles.

California's new "No Robo Bosses" law, signed September 30, 2026, limits how employers use software to discipline or fire workers. Its author's announcement and a law-firm summary describe discipline and firing. Neither mentions scheduling.

## What is not known

The Shift Project surveyed workers at very large chains. The Daily Nun did not find comparable data on high-school students who work part time.

## The short version

- Employers decide who works and when. Many hourly workers get less than two weeks' notice.
- California requires some pay when a shift is cut short, and caps hours for workers under 18.
- Rules on advance notice exist in some cities, not everywhere.
