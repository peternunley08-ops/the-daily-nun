# 4. Who owns the media?

The question sounds as if it should produce a short list of names. Instead, it produces a second question: what counts as media? A movie studio, a local radio station, an investigative nonprofit, a newspaper subscription business, a video-sharing service and a connected television platform can all affect public attention. They do not produce the same product, sell access in the same way or compete for exactly the same audience. Combining their logos into one diagram can reveal corporate connections, but it cannot by itself measure a market.

This is the central difficulty with the claim that four companies own 90% of all media. This investigation did not establish a reproducible, current statistical foundation for that sentence. It did establish a traceable earlier version involving six corporations, along with evidence of substantial corporate ownership and important recent mergers. Those are different findings. Rejecting an undefined percentage does not mean rejecting the concern about concentrated power. [O01] [O02] [O04]

## Following the claim backward

An accessible stop on the claim's journey is Ashley Lutz's 2012 Business Insider article, whose headline says six corporations control 90% of American media. It attributes an infographic to Jason at Frugal Dad. The article itself warns that the graphic was already from the previous year and had missed significant transactions. That warning is unusually revealing: an image that appears to offer a stable map of an industry was circulating with acknowledged errors while being used to support an enormous aggregate claim. [O01]

The article does not supply a transparent calculation in which a defined list of American media properties forms the denominator, a consistent ownership rule determines the numerator and a measurement date anchors both. Its warning is evidence that the ownership list needed updating. It is not evidence that the 90% figure was correctly measured in the first place. Nor does an updated list of six, five or four corporate parents repair a missing denominator. This report can trace a recognizable six-company version to that accessible article and its attribution. It cannot establish who first changed it into the four-company version or certify the earliest appearance of every variant. [O01]

Consider four possible meanings of a media share. A share of advertising revenue measures commercial receipts in a defined advertising market. A share of audience time measures attention, usually within a particular population and medium. A share of outlets counts properties, treating a small station and a large national network according to a counting rule. A share of original reporting measures something harder still: the actual production of information that others reuse. Each can answer an important question. None can substitute for the others without explanation.

There is a further problem with the word "control." Owning a company usually carries different rights from owning a minority stake, licensing a show, selling advertisements around someone else's reporting, carrying a television channel or recommending a creator's video. A distributor may strongly influence an outlet's revenue without owning its newsroom. An owner may set budgets and appoint leaders without dictating every article. An investigation should identify the mechanism it is testing instead of treating every commercial relationship as identical editorial command.

Several observations follow directly from these records. Paramount's acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery was announced as completed on October 6, 2026, and the combined company was named Skydance. A current discussion should therefore not assume that CBS News and CNN necessarily sit under separate corporate parents because an older chart puts them there. The company's forecasts of better competition, financial savings or creative opportunity remain its own claims; the closing itself does not establish those hoped-for benefits. [O02]

Conversely, separation can make an old chart overstate a connection. Versant's November 2026 release dates its separation from Comcast to January 2. NBCUniversal's current portfolio identifies NBC News and Peacock, while Versant identifies CNBC and MS NOW. Repeating an older Comcast cable-network list without noticing the separation would misdescribe the corporate relationship. A spin-off creates a different ownership structure, though it does not by itself prove stronger journalism, a more diverse audience or less partisan programming. [O03] [O06]

Fox's completed acquisition of Roku offers a different kind of connection: ownership across content and a connected-TV distribution platform. The relevant democratic questions concern incentives, access and treatment of rivals. Will a distributor favor related products? What commitments govern that conduct? What do audience and competition data show? The company promises to preserve an open ecosystem. That commitment is worth recording and testing over time, rather than accepting as proof that no conflict can arise or assuming in advance that every rival will be excluded. [O04]

## Ownership is more than a logo

The TikTok example demonstrates why a national label can be inadequate. Its U.S. joint-venture announcement describes particular responsibilities for user data, algorithm security and moderation, while assigning global interoperability and some commercial activities to other TikTok entities. It says ByteDance retains 19.9% of the joint venture's equity. That percentage has an explicit denominator: the U.S. venture's equity, not global TikTok, all short-video content or American media generally. A smaller stake and a different operating arrangement can matter without answering every question about technical dependence, licensing or influence. [O09]

The same care applies to familiar technology companies. Alphabet's creation separated a holding-company identity from Google's consumer-facing identity, while YouTube retained its own brand. A person watching a video may recognize the creator, recognize YouTube and never think about the parent company. That layering creates several places where power can operate: the creator's editorial choices, the platform's recommendation system, its advertising rules and the parent's investment decisions. Corporate ownership explains one layer; it does not erase the others. [O12] [O13] [M06]

Newspapers and radio require their own maps. News Corp's publishing portfolio is not identical to Fox's broadcast and streaming portfolio. The New York Times Company's reporting operation is not a subsidiary of the entertainment groups in the old infographic. iHeartMedia's portfolio concerns radio, networks, audio advertising and podcasts. A single entertainment-industry ownership image cannot establish the concentration of local newspaper reporting or radio listening in a specific city. The correct next step is to examine that sector and location, using a consistent set of competitors and measures. [O05] [O07] [O08]

Ownership can also matter through financial structure. A business that must service debt may cut spending, seek higher margins or sell properties. A wealthy owner may finance expensive reporting, impose a personal preference, or do both in different areas. A publicly traded company can face commercial pressures even when no individual owner gives a political instruction. These are plausible mechanisms to investigate; identifying a business model is the beginning of a causal explanation, not evidence that a particular decision occurred for that reason.

For democratic accountability, ask what the structure makes possible and what the record shows was actually done. Who appoints the editor? Who decides how many reporters cover a statehouse? Can managers require a message to run across stations? Is a distribution deal capable of threatening an outlet's survival? What formal or informal protections let journalists challenge those pressures? Those questions connect ownership to observable decisions. They are more demanding than counting logos, but they produce explanations that can be checked.

## What the table can and cannot settle

An ownership table is not an editorial-bias scorecard. Two outlets under one parent may maintain different staffs, audiences and editorial practices. Outlets with different owners may reuse the same wire report, react to the same event or copy one another's formats. Identical wording can be evidence of a corporate directive in a documented case; it can also arise from syndication, attribution to a shared source or routine repetition of an official statement. The mechanism and context determine what an example establishes.

Nor should the number of available websites be mistaken for the number of independently funded reporting operations. A city can have many social accounts repeating the work of one shrinking newsroom. A platform can offer millions of videos while important government meetings go uncovered. Equally, a person can publish valuable original reporting outside a large corporate chain. The meaningful question is whether citizens can obtain reliable, sufficiently independent information about institutions they can influence, not whether the internet contains many pages.

The conclusion is therefore specific. Large corporations own important portions of entertainment, broadcasting, publishing and digital distribution. Recent completed transactions and separations change that landscape. The reviewed evidence does not justify turning these facts into an undefined four-companies-own-90% statistic. A useful investigation keeps the concern about concentrated power and improves the measurement: name the market, name the properties, distinguish ownership from distribution, state the date and test the consequences. Democracy needs that precision because the remedies for a local reporting shortage, a national merger and a recommendation-system problem are different.
