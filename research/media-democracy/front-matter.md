# A guide to this report

This report begins with a familiar experience: recognizing the political argument in a headline without necessarily knowing the institutions, incentives, or evidence behind it. It is written for curious readers, including a high-school community whose members can disagree sharply while still benefiting from better questions. Its purpose is to make those questions easier to investigate, not to assign readers a political identity.

The central question is who influences what Americans hear, see, and believe. The answer requires following several kinds of power. A corporate owner can influence resources and leadership. A newsroom decides what to investigate and how to establish a claim. A platform can influence discovery and circulation without owning the reporting. Audiences choose sources, interpret messages, and share them with others. Keeping these roles distinct makes criticism more precise.

## What the evidence supports

- Common ownership can produce common instructions and measurable changes in coverage. The documented Sinclair episode and research on local television acquisitions establish more than a similarity between headlines, while leaving persuasion and broader generalization as separate questions. [H01] [H02]
- The familiar claim that four companies own 90% of all media lacks a reproducible definition in the sources traced here. Corporate concentration is real, but an ownership map needs a sector, measure, and date. Recent completed mergers and separations make old lists unreliable. [O01] [O02] [O03] [O04]
- Platforms influence the information people encounter. That does not mean every change in a feed changes attitudes in the same way. Experiments have found meaningful effects in some settings and small or undetected effects on specified outcomes in others. [E01] [E02] [E03] [E15] [M09]
- Policy disagreement, dislike of opposing voters, division among political leaders, and perceptions of division are different problems. Media belong among the explanations to examine alongside identity, institutions, geography, and political conditions. [M12] [M13] [M14]
- A democracy can have abundant political commentary and too little reporting on consequential local decisions. Evidence on coverage, voter knowledge, public finance, and local-news availability makes reporting capacity a separate concern from online moderation. [H14] [E06] [E08]

## How to use the evidence

The numbered citations are clickable and lead to the underlying source. The bibliography supplies authors, dates, publication details, source types, and access limitations. A survey percentage measures a specified population and question; it is not automatically a measure of accuracy, control, or persuasion. A company statement establishes what the company announced or says its policy is. It does not independently establish that the policy works.

This is an AI-assisted research synthesis prepared for Peter Nunley and The Daily Nun, not original interviews, a new survey, a systematic review, or a meta-analysis. The ten chapters contain roughly 16,400 words before this guide, glossary, and bibliography. The research uses 54 distinct sources; some original studies were accessible only through abstracts and metadata, and those limits are recorded. Legal decisions are discussed as dated holdings, not an exhaustive audit of later litigation.

The intended event cutoff is November 11, 2026; compilation and source checks occurred on November 12. Dated studies retain their original measurement periods. Undated corporate pages are current-access corroboration, not archived proof of their exact contents on the cutoff date. The ownership chapter flags older records and secondary-source cross-checks. Conclusions distinguish established findings, plausible mechanisms, and questions that remain open.
